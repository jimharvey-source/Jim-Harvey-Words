# Jim Harvey Words: the first read

Read on 7 September 2026. All 87 poems in the archive. Verdict first, ledger after.

## The verdict

You are not a love poet. The archive says you think you are, because more than half of it is heartbreak. But the heartbreak poems that stay in the head, with no address and no object, are the weakest things here. "She was born to hurt", "When they are soul-dead", "The red sun", "Walk away" could have been written by anyone who has been left. The ones that work have a bench in Russell Square, a card cut into confetti, a list found on the way to the tube, a rainy night in Belsize Park. The rule that falls out of the whole archive is this: when you name the street and the person, the poem lives. When you name the feeling, it dies.

So the lane is not love. The lane is London, and the people who made you, told by a man in his sixties who does not flinch and does not pose. Green Chair, Southampton Row, Primrose Hill, Dagenham Toilet, Russell Square, Txt, The Tree, Til it made me sad to see flowers, If I should die today. Put those nine together and a reader knows exactly who is talking. Nobody else on Instagram sounds like that. Plenty of people sound like "The wound".

Your signature is the one-word ending. "Unsold." "Flowers." "Solid." "Not in jail." That is the thing to protect. You set up a stanza of detail and drop one word on it. It is the same move you teach clients: the pause, then the line. Keep doing it and never explain it.

Your best register is the deadpan. "A lovely boy, talks too much and lazy." "Well 'it' ended, evidently, in a field in Slough, just short of terminal 2." "And all my hopes for filth just wound up dead." That is you. The Buddhist ego poem and the Newton poem are you trying to be wise, and wisdom is not your gift. Seeing is.

Three habits to break.

First, the comma at the end of every line. "Floorboards trodden smooth by all those feet, / Of all the lives that passed through here since when, / This mansion block". The commas fight the syntax. Let the line break do the work and punctuate the sentence as a sentence. This one edit improves twenty poems.

Second, rhyme you have to bend the line to reach. "Avoid it / noisy", "answers / cancer", "amaze me / lazy" (that one earns it, the others do not). Your unrhymed pentameter is better than your rhymed quatrains. Rhyme only when it arrives.

Third, borrowed lines. "That's where the light gets in" is Leonard Cohen's, cut it. "Sasha Kissed Me" is Leigh Hunt's "Jenny Kissed Me" with new furniture, which is a fine thing to do so long as the credit line says "after Leigh Hunt". The "experimental" second half of September Song reads as machine-written, and so does the pasted review under Science. Neither goes on the site. Your own first stanza of September Song is better than anything that follows it.

One rule on people. Paul, Antonella, Jayne, Emma and the man with the belt are real. The attack poems stay in the drawer for good: they are score-settling, they are legally exposed, and they make the reader side with the target. The occasional poems for friends (Solid, Twins, Becky) go out only with a nod from the subject. Sasha, if she is real, gets asked.

On length, the archive settles the channel question. The good poems run twelve to thirty lines. That is an email, not a grid square. Instagram gets the handful of four-to-eight-liners whole (Four Numbers in Soho, Russell Square, In dreams remembered, Dreams, Playlist, She came for a season, She is made of love) and gets an extract plus a link for everything else. Newsletter first, as agreed.

Songs are a separate pile. Fifteen of these files are lyrics, some good. They belong with your songwriting, not on the poetry site. A lyric on the page without its tune looks thin, and readers judge it as a thin poem.

A working title for the collection, offered once: Passing Clouds. A London cigarette brand, mortality, and the way the poems drift over named places. Yours to keep or bin.

## What happens next

Pile one is 29 poems. That is the shortlist, and it arrived without forcing. Every one needs the comma edit and a proofread, nothing more. Pile two is 15 poems worth finishing, and Primrose Hill is the one to finish first: it is the biggest thing you have written and the back half is still notes. Pile three is 43 files: songs, fragments, duplicates, borrowed lines, and the drawer.

Next step: I edit pile one, poem by poem, and send them back to you in batches of five for approval. You finish Primrose Hill by hand. Nothing gets built until the 29 are clean.

## The rubric

Every poem was read against five questions. Image: can I see it? Voice: does it sound like you and nobody else? Turn: does the ending earn its place? Finish: is it done? Sendable: would a reader forward it or write back? A poem needs four of five for pile one, two or three with a clear fix for pile two.

## Addendum: the older Word files (added 7 September, later)

Five Word files arrived after the first read. "3 Things You Do" is a business list, not poetry, and sits outside this project. "Poems for review 2" and "Poems for illustration" repeat poems already in the archive, with one exception below. Eight duplicates are noted in the ledger and not re-scored. Seven new poems follow.

The footnote under Southampton Row settles the Teheran question: it is the Persian version of the Appointment in Samarra story. Keep the poem's last line and put the proverb under it as a two-line note, exactly as you had it.

Night Time, Post Script and Paper & Ink change the shape of the collection. They are the centre of it. Night Time is the best poem you have written: the rounds of the bedrooms, "two blonde heads", "seven, two, and one who's gone for good", and a last stanza that refuses to agonise and so makes the reader do it. Paper & Ink is nearly as good, and "four times as long as Joseph lived, just waiting" is the line the whole collection will be remembered for. Post Script breaks off mid-sentence and needs either finishing or cutting to its first two stanzas. Whether these three go public is your decision and nobody else's. If they do, they sit together, in the middle, and nothing goes either side of them for a page.
